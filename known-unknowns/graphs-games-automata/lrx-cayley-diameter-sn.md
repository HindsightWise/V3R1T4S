# Diameter of the Cayley graph of S_n with generators (1,2) and the two n-cycle rotations (LRX): prove n(n-1)/2, or compute a(16) exactly

🕸️ **Graphs, games and automata** · standard · tractability ★★☆☆☆ · compute: needs cluster (an n = 16 BFS over 16! = 2.1e13 states needs about 5 TB at 2 bits per state); the main proof is pure thought plus Lean · open as of 2026-10-30

## The problem

OEIS A186783 gives the diameter of the Cayley graph of S_n generated by the transposition (1,2) and the rotations (1,2,...,n)^{±1} (the 'LRX' graph). The OEIS data run to n = 13: 0, 1, 2, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28, 36, 45, 55, 66, 78. These come from Bartoletti, Hardin, Li and Bieron, with a(13) by Ben Whitmore in 2018. The conjecture a(n) = n(n-1)/2 for n > 3 (Alexandersson 2020, in the OEIS entry) is open for general n. Antiufeev (arXiv 2601.08715, v3 June 2026) proved a(n) >= n(n-1)/2, so the open part is the matching upper bound. The best proven upper bound is n(n-1)/2 + 3n (Chervov, Soibelman, Lytkin et al., 'CayleyPy RL', arXiv 2502.18663, 2025). That paper also reports full BFS traversals of the LRX graphs for n <= 15 confirming that the longest element has length n(n-1)/2, so the conjecture is computationally settled through n = 15 (not yet in the OEIS data). It also conjectures that the unique longest element is (2,1,n,n-1,...,3), a conjecture Dmitry Kamenetsky recorded in the OEIS. The same paper gives an explicit word of length n(n-1)/2 for that element for every n, and its diffusion-distance pipelines and a Kaggle challenge (n = 2..200) found no shorter word. Those bound only that element's distance, not the diameter.

## What counts as a win

Success means a machine-checked proof (Lean, Coq or Isabelle) that the LRX diameter is at most n(n-1)/2, and hence equals n(n-1)/2, for all n >= 4. A counterexample also counts: an n >= 16 and a permutation whose distance exceeds n(n-1)/2, certified by an exhaustive BFS that the skeptic re-runs, or by a machine-checked distance lower-bound proof.

## Partial wins

- **p1:** The exact value a(16) from a complete, re-runnable BFS of the 16! vertices (layer sizes published and summing to 16!), provided no published source already reports it.
- **p2:** A machine-checked proof of an upper bound n(n-1)/2 + c*n for all n with c < 3, improving the 2025 bound n(n-1)/2 + 3n.
- **p3:** A machine-checked proof (Lean, Coq or Isabelle) of the known lower bound a(n) >= n(n-1)/2 for all n > 3 (Antiufeev 2026), as the first half of a formal proof of the conjecture.

## How a win is checked

For BFS claims: re-run the released BFS (bit-packed visited set) on independent hardware and compare the layer sizes, which must sum to n!, and the final depth. For proofs: run the proof assistant's checker on the released file.

## Traps

False wins others have fallen for. A claim that walks into one of these will not survive the skeptics.

- a(14) and a(15) are already settled by the full BFS reported in arXiv 2502.18663 (n <= 15), even though the OEIS data stop at n = 13; do not claim them.
- Word lengths found by beam search, diffusion distance, Kaggle solutions or GAP for one element are upper bounds for that element, not the diameter.
- The CayleyPy group runs large GPU BFS computations; check their latest papers and OEIS before claiming a(16).
