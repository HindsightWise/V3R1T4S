# Borsuk's conjecture: a finite counterexample in dimension 62 or less (finite record 63, Grinsztajn 2026)

✏️ **Erdős problems and proofs** · standard · tractability ★★☆☆☆ · compute: laptop-hours (construction search over strongly regular graphs and two-distance sets; verification minutes) · open as of 2026-10-30

## The problem

Borsuk asked whether every bounded set in R^d can be split into d+1 parts of smaller diameter. For a finite set F this number b(F) is the chromatic number of its diameter graph (pairs at maximum distance). Kahn and Kalai (1993) disproved the conjecture in dimension 1325. Later records: Nilli 946, Raigorodskii 561, Weissbach 560, Hinrichs 323, Pikhurko 321, Hinrichs-Richter 298, then the two-distance sets of Bondarenko (65, 2014) and Jenrich-Brouwer (64, 352 points, 2014). In May 2026 Max Grinsztajn (with GPT-5.5 Pro; github.com/maaxgrin/borsuk-63-counterexample) gave a 321-point subset of R^63, built from the G2(4) strongly regular graph (416 vertices). Its smaller-diameter subsets have at most 5 points, so at least 65 parts are needed. Tao's optimisation-constants page C28 lists 63 as the best non-starred bound. OpenAI's preprint 'A nine-dimensional counterexample to Borsuk's covering assertion' (23 Sep 2026; unrefereed, asterisked on Tao's page) claims b(X) > 10 for a COMPACT INFINITE set in R^9, the rank-one projectors on R^4. Its proof is topological and gives no finite set. Parts of smaller diameter must stay bounded away from the diameter, so a compact counterexample does not automatically yield a finite one; Wikipedia notes this gap. The conjecture holds for d <= 3. Target: a finite point set in R^d with d <= 62 and b > d+1.

## What counts as a win

An explicit finite set X in R^d with d <= 62, given exactly (rational or number-field coordinates, or an exact Gram matrix certified positive semidefinite with rank <= d), together with a certificate that its diameter graph G (pairs at the exact maximum distance) is not (d+1)-colourable: either a computed independence number alpha(G) with |X| > (d+1)*alpha(G), where alpha(G) is certified by an exact exhaustive search or an LP/SDP dual bound in exact arithmetic, or a DRAT/LRAT-checked UNSAT proof of (d+1)-colourability. d must be STRICTLY LESS (direction: min) than the smallest dimension of a FINITE counterexample listed on https://teorth.github.io/optimizationproblems/constants/28a.html, on the Wikipedia Borsuk's conjecture page, and in a fresh check at verification time (63 on 2026-10-30). Compact or infinite constructions do not count.

## Ideas to start from

Starting points, not instructions. The best wins came from angles nobody listed.

- Grinsztajn's dimension drop: split the strongly regular graph's vertex set so that one part's span loses a dimension, then keep only points whose independence bound survives. Try the same split on other strongly regular graphs with small eigenvalue multiplicity (Brouwer's tables).
- Finite subsets of the OpenAI projector set: sample many lines in R^4 (or R^k for larger k), take the projectors uu^T, and look for a finite subset whose orthogonality graph has chromatic number above d+1 for the embedding dimension d = k(k+1)/2 - 1.
- Two-distance sets from Euclidean representations of strongly regular graphs: dimension = an eigenvalue multiplicity; need |X|/alpha > multiplicity + 1.

## Traps

False wins others have fallen for. A claim that walks into one of these will not survive the skeptics.

- Dimension bookkeeping: points on a sphere in R^(m+1) lying in an affine hyperplane live in R^m; points with an exact linear dependency drop further. Compute the exact affine rank.
- Floating-point diameter: the diameter graph must use exact equality with the maximum distance.
- The 9-dimensional OpenAI result is a compact infinite set and does not count; any finite subset must be certified on its own.
- For finite sets b(F) equals the chromatic number of the diameter graph only; edges at non-maximal distances are irrelevant.
